# Plain language — government audiences only

**Load this file only when the reader is a U.S. federal, state, or local
government one.** Everything in it derives from the Plain Writing Act of 2010 and
the Federal Plain Language Guidelines, which were written for agency writers
addressing the public. Several of these rules are actively wrong elsewhere: §G6
would flatten an essay's headings into signage, and the substitution table would
strip a novelist's vocabulary. If the audience is not a government one, close
this file and use the register profile alone.

It sits alongside the Regulated register in `reference/registers.md`, which
carries the condensed floor. Nothing here replaces a pattern in the catalog. The
36 patterns, the always-on 21 and the gated 15, are unchanged by this file.

---

## 1 — What the Act requires

The Plain Writing Act of 2010, Pub. L. 111-274, enacted 13 October 2010.

- **Plain writing** is defined as writing that is clear, concise, well organized,
  and follows best practice appropriate to the subject or field and intended
  audience (§3).
- **Covered documents** are the documents a member of the public needs to obtain
  a federal benefit or service, to file taxes, or to comply with a federal
  requirement — letters, forms, notices, instructions, publications, and their
  electronic equivalents (§3).
- **Regulations are excluded** from the definition of a covered document (§3).
  A rule in the Federal Register is not what this Act reaches. Executive Orders
  13563 and 12866 speak to regulatory clarity instead.
- Agencies must designate a responsible official, train staff, and publish
  compliance reports (§4, §5).
- **§6 forecloses judicial review.** No right of action arises under the Act.

**What follows from §6, and it matters for the delivery.** There is no finding to
pass and no certificate to hold. The defensible claim is *"written to the Federal
Plain Language Guidelines"*. Never write, and never let a draft claim, "Plain
Writing Act compliant" — it asserts a compliance status the statute does not
create.

## 4 — §G1 to §G7

Seven rules the catalog does not carry. The `G` prefix is deliberate: these are
not patterns §37 onward, they are a government-scoped set that a non-government
run never loads. Detect mode may cite them by number.

### §G1 — Hidden verbs

An action turned into a noun, propped up by a weak verb. The Guidelines call
these hidden verbs; grammarians call them nominalizations. They add words, bury
the action, and pair almost automatically with passive voice.

The tell is a verb ending in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*, *-ency*, *-ity* or *-al*
sitting after *make*, *take*, *give*, *provide*, *conduct*, *perform*, *achieve*,
*effect*, *undertake* or *is/are in*.

> **Before.** The agency will make a determination on your application and will
> provide notification of the decision. A review of the file is conducted prior
> to the issuance of a final approval.

> **After.** We will decide on your application and tell you the result. We
> review your file before approving it.

Two sentences, 34 words down to 17, and the actor is now visible in both.

### §G2 — Noun strings

Three or more nouns stacked into a phrase, each modifying the next. The reader
has to guess which noun governs which, and different readers guess differently.

> **Before.** Draft laboratory animal facility management improvement plan
> submission requirements.

> **After.** Requirements for submitting a draft plan to improve how you manage
> laboratory animal facilities.

Break the string with prepositions and verbs. Longer is correct here: the
original is shorter only because it made the reader do the work.

**Two nouns are usually fine.** *Application form*, *filing deadline*. The rule
starts biting at three, and a run of four is almost always ambiguous.

### §G3 — Positive language

State what *is*, and what the reader *must* do. Negatives cost the reader a
mental inversion each, and they compound.

- **Double negatives.** "Not unless you have not filed" — count the negations,
  then rewrite them away.
- **Exceptions to exceptions.** A rule, then an exception, then an exception to
  the exception. Rewrite as the set of cases that actually apply.
- **Hidden negatives.** *unless*, *except*, *fail to*, *other than*, *unlawful*,
  *notwithstanding* carry a negation without a *not*.

> **Before.** No application will be considered ineligible unless the applicant
> has failed to submit documentation other than that listed in Appendix B.

> **After.** Your application is eligible if you submit every document listed in
> Appendix B.

**This is not §9.** §9 is negative parallelism — the rhetorical *"it's not just
X, it's Y"* — and it is an AI tell in any register. §G3 is about logical
negation making a government instruction hard to follow. The two share a word and
nothing else.

### §G4 — Main idea before its exceptions

Put the rule first, then its conditions, exceptions and cross-references. A
reader who meets three conditions before learning what they condition has to hold
all three in mind with nothing to attach them to.

> **Before.** Except as provided in paragraph (c), and unless the Administrator
> determines otherwise under §401.7, an applicant who has previously filed under
> this part must submit Form 21 within 30 days.

> **After.** You must submit Form 21 within 30 days if you have filed under this
> part before. Two exceptions apply — see paragraph (c) and §401.7.

Where a mandated template fixes the order, the template wins. Say so rather than
silently leaving the sentence inverted.

### §G5 — Keep subject, verb and object together

Long interruptions between the subject and its verb, or between the verb and its
object, force the reader to hold the sentence open. This is why a 40-word
sentence can be readable and a 22-word one impossible.

> **Before.** The applicant, after reviewing all materials described in the
> preceding paragraph and consulting with the designated regional
> representative, must sign the form.

> **After.** The applicant must sign the form after reviewing the materials in
> the previous paragraph and consulting the regional representative.

This rule and the 30-word cap in Step 4.2 fix different faults. A sentence can
pass the cap and still fail this one.

### §G6 — Useful headings

The Guidelines recognise three heading types, and want the reader to be able to
navigate by heading alone.

| Type | Use it for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Question** | Public-facing material where the reader arrives with a question | *How do I appeal a decision?* |
| **Statement** | Instructions and requirements | *File your appeal within 30 days* |
| **Topic** | Reference material and short sections only | *Appeals* |

Prefer question and statement headings; topic headings carry the least
information and are the easiest default to fall into. Keep one type consistent
within a document, and never let a heading be the only place a fact appears —
§29's rule that a heading is not a sentence of the prose still applies.

**Title case stays off** in Regulated, per §17. That gate is not relaxed here.

### §G7 — Definitions and abbreviations, kept few

- **Define only what genuinely needs defining,** in the place the reader meets it
  rather than in a block at the front. A definitions section the reader must
  hold in memory is a cost paid on every page.
- **Never define a word into a meaning it does not have.** If the definition
  fights ordinary usage, change the word instead.
- **Abbreviate only what recurs often enough to earn it.** A term used twice
  should be written out twice. A page of initialisms defined once at the top is
  the failure mode this rule exists to prevent.
- **Spell out on first use**, then use the abbreviation consistently — which is
  §11, not a new rule, and it is already inverted for Regulated.

## 6 — Testing with real readers

The Guidelines close with the check this skill cannot perform. Stating the
boundary is part of the delivery, not a disclaimer to skip.

- **Paraphrase testing.** Give the document to six to nine people from the real
  audience, one at a time, and ask them to say in their own words what each
  section requires of them. Where the paraphrase is wrong, the writing is wrong —
  regardless of how it scores on every rule above.
- **Usability testing.** Give them the task the document exists to support and
  watch whether they complete it.

Six to nine interviews is where the Guidelines put the point of diminishing
return. Both methods find faults no editing pass can: a correct sentence the
reader interprets backwards, a step nobody realises applies to them.

**No amount of rule-following substitutes for it, and this skill has not done
it.** Say that in the delivery.

## 7 — Out of scope, named

The delivery must state that these were not checked. They are real obligations
and none of them is covered here.

- **Section 508 / accessibility.** Reading order, alt text, table structure, colour
  contrast, tagged PDFs.
- **GPO Style Manual.** Federal capitalization, abbreviation and citation
  conventions, which differ from ordinary editorial style.
- **Agency style guides.** They override the Guidelines where they conflict, and
  a plain-language rewrite that contradicts one is a rework.
- **Mandated section structures.** RFP response outlines, SSP control formats,
  ATO package contents. Never restructure one to satisfy §G4 or §G6.
- **21st Century IDEA** (Pub. L. 115-336) — website and digital service
  modernization obligations.
